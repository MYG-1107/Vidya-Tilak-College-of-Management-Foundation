Generative AI tools can help people draft, summarise, brainstorm, classify and explore information. For business professionals, the useful question is not simply which tool to use. It is **which task should be improved, what information can be shared, how the result will be checked and who remains accountable**.

A practical generative AI course should teach workflows and judgement, not only prompt examples.

## Business tasks where AI may assist

Depending on the tool, data policy and business context, teams may explore AI support for:

- Drafting first versions of internal documents.
- Summarising non-confidential material.
- Creating outlines for training or presentations.
- Generating ideas for customer research.
- Organising feedback themes for human review.
- Supporting routine knowledge searches.
- Preparing draft code or spreadsheet explanations for validation.

These are potential uses, not guarantees of accuracy or productivity. The value should be measured against the actual task.

## Skills to learn

A useful learning sequence includes:

1. **Task definition:** describe the audience, objective, inputs and success criteria.
2. **Prompt design:** provide context, constraints, format and examples where appropriate.
3. **Source checking:** verify claims against reliable material.
4. **Iteration:** refine outputs and record what changed.
5. **Workflow design:** decide where a human review or approval is required.
6. **Risk awareness:** consider privacy, intellectual property, bias, security and reliability.

A strong exercise asks learners to compare an AI-generated draft with a verified source and mark unsupported statements.

## What should never be assumed?

An AI answer is not automatically true, current, fair or legally compliant. Do not place confidential business data, personal information, passwords or restricted material into a tool unless the organisation has approved that use. Do not rely on generated content as legal, medical, financial or safety advice without qualified review.

For risk-management concepts, the [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) is a useful public reference. Organisations should also follow applicable laws and their own internal policies.

## How to evaluate an AI course

| Course element | What good coverage looks like |
|---|---|
| Foundations | Explains what generative AI can and cannot do |
| Prompting | Teaches structured, task-specific prompts |
| Verification | Requires evidence checks and human review |
| Business context | Uses realistic examples relevant to teams |
| Privacy and safety | Discusses confidential data, bias and security |
| Projects | Produces a documented workflow or evaluated output |
| Updates | Explains that tools and capabilities change |

## Generative AI for managers vs technical AI study

A manager-focused course may prioritise use-case selection, governance, workflow design and evaluating business value. Technical AI study may go deeper into programming, statistics, machine learning and model development. Choose based on the work you plan to do.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do business professionals need coding to learn generative AI?

Not for every application. However, technical roles may require programming and deeper data skills. Check the course's intended level.

### Can AI write a complete business report?

It can produce a draft, but the author must verify data, references, calculations, context and conclusions. Accountability remains with the person or organisation publishing it.

### Does an AI certificate guarantee a job?

No. Employers assess demonstrated capability, experience and role requirements. A portfolio showing safe and useful work is more informative than an unsupported claim.
